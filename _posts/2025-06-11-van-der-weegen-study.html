The study It's LiFe! Mobile and web-based monitoring and feedback tool embedded in primary care increases physical activity: a cluster randomized controlled trial by Van der Weegen et al. (2015) examined the effectiveness of It's LiFe!, a digital intervention that combines a mobile/web-based monitoring tool and nurse couseling (pictured in figure 1), in hopes of increasing physical activity in patients living with COPD or type 2 diabetes in primary care. The cluster randomized controlled trial appointed 24 Dutch primary care practices to three groups:

  • Full intervention (activity monitor, abb/web portal, and nurse couseling)
  • Only nurse counseling
  • Usual care

The tool offered real-time tracking of physical activity, goal-setting, and feedback, while nurses provided organized couseling. The course of the intervention trials can be viewed in figure 2 below. The primary result (minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per day) was measured using accelerometers. Outcomes exhibited that the full intervention group increased physical activity by 12 minutes per day in comparison to usual care, which sustained for three months post-intervention. By comparison, counseling alone (no digital tool) had no significant effect, which emphasizes the potentially important role of the digital intervention in changing behavioural patterns. Self-efficacy, on the other hand, exhibited no improvements, although quality of mental health improved for a short time post-intervention.

This study stands out in Western et al.'s meta-analysis by showing noticeable increases in physical activity in high socioeconomic status patients through a hybrid human-digital design approach. Its strengths include the use of objective physical activity tracking, in-person primary care integration, and user-centered design. In addition to this, by comparison to other studies observed in the meta-analysis, it is interesting to see efforts being made here to maintain and perhaps even improve physical activity and the overall well-being of individuals who are already at a disadvantage in a health-related sense. Interventions like these may provide much-needed encouragement to these groups to maximize control over their own lives. Nevertheless, this study also has its limitations, such as a relatively short follow-up and not accounting for specific low socioeconomic status needs (as implicated by the lack of change in physical activity in low socioeconomic status groups). Overall, this study offers compelling insight into a hybrid human-digital approach to physical activity interventions, though more engagement and adjustments with similar patients of lower socioeconomic status is absolutely necessary in the future.
