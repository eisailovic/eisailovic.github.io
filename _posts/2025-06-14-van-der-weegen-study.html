Mobile and web-based monitoring and feedback tool embedded in primary care increases physical activity: a cluster randomized controlled trial" background: '/img/vecteezy_a-stack-of-magazines-is-shown-in-this-photo_30749912.jpg' date: 2025-06-14 author: Emilia Isailovic ---

The study by Van der Weegen et al. (2015) examined the effectiveness of It's LiFe!, a digital intervention that combines a mobile/web-based monitoring tool and nurse couseling, in hopes of increasing physical activity in patients living with COPD or type 2 diabetes in primary care. The cluster randomized controlled trial appointed 24 Dutch primary care practices to three groups:

  • Full intervention (activity monitor, abb/web portal, and nurse couseling)
  • Only nurse counseling
  • Usual care

The tool offered real-time tracking of physical activity, goal-setting, and feedback, while nurses provided organized couseling. The primary result (minutes of moderate-to-vigorous physical activity per day) was measured using accelerometers. Outcomes exhibited that the full intervention group increased physical activity by 12 minutes per day in comparison to usual care, which sustained for three months post-intervention. By comparison, counseling alone (no digital tool) had no significant effect, which emphasizes the potentially important role of the digital intervention in changing behavioural patterns. Self-efficacy, on the other hand, exhibited no improvements, although quality of mental health improved for a short time post-intervention.

This study stands out in Western et al.'s meta-analysis by showing noticeable increases in physical activity in high socioeconomic status patients by means of a hybrid human-digital design approach. Its strengths include the use of objective physical activity tracking, in-person primary care integration, and user-centered design. Nevertheless, this study also has its limitations, such as a relatively short follow-up and not accounting for specific low socioeconomic status needs (as implicated by the lack of change in physical activity in low socioeconomic status groups), further indicating that even digital tools that are integrated clinically may not be enough to close gaps.
